## Describing oscillations

An **oscillation** is a repeated movement to and fro about a fixed point, called the **equilibrium position**, where the resultant force on the object is zero. A mass on a spring, a pendulum and a vibrating guitar string are all oscillators.

<Definition term="Displacement, amplitude, period and frequency">
The **displacement** $x$ is the distance of the object from the equilibrium position, in a stated direction. The **amplitude** $x_0$ is the maximum displacement. The **period** $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation. The **frequency** $f$ is the number of complete oscillations per unit time.
</Definition>

<KeyEquation
  tex="f = \dfrac{1}{T} \qquad\qquad \omega = 2\pi f = \dfrac{2\pi}{T}"
  where="f | frequency | Hz
T | period | s
\omega | angular frequency | rad s⁻¹" />

The angular frequency $\omega$ is the same idea as angular velocity in circular motion. One oscillation corresponds to $2\pi$ radians of phase.

## Simple harmonic motion

<Definition term="Simple harmonic motion (SHM)">
Simple harmonic motion is an oscillation in which the acceleration of the object is directly proportional to its displacement from a fixed point, and is always directed towards that point.
</Definition>

This is written as one equation. The minus sign says the acceleration is **opposite** to the displacement.

<KeyEquation
  tex="a = -\omega^2 x"
  where="a | acceleration | m s⁻²
\omega | angular frequency | rad s⁻¹
x | displacement from equilibrium | m" />

If the object starts at the equilibrium position, the solutions are:

<KeyEquation
  tex="x = x_0 \sin \omega t \qquad\quad v = v_0 \cos \omega t \qquad\quad v_0 = \omega x_0"
  where="x | displacement at time t | m
x_0 | amplitude | m
v | velocity at time t | m s⁻¹
v_0 | maximum speed | m s⁻¹
t | time | s" />

The speed at any displacement is given by the next equation. It is largest at $x = 0$ (giving $v_0 = \omega x_0$) and zero at $x = \pm x_0$:

<KeyEquation
  tex="v = \pm\,\omega\sqrt{x_0^2 - x^2}"
  where="v | speed at displacement x | m s⁻¹
\omega | angular frequency | rad s⁻¹
x_0 | amplitude | m
x | displacement | m" />

Three facts to remember from the graphs of $x$, $v$ and $a$ against time:

- At the **ends** of the motion ($x = \pm x_0$): the speed is zero and the acceleration is at its **maximum**, $a_0 = \omega^2 x_0$.
- At the **equilibrium position** ($x = 0$): the speed is at its **maximum** and the acceleration is **zero**.
- The velocity graph is a **quarter of a cycle ahead** of the displacement graph, and the acceleration graph is **in antiphase** with the displacement graph.

### Two common oscillators

For a mass $m$ on a spring of spring constant $k$, the force is $F = -kx$, so $ma = -kx$, which matches $a = -\omega^2 x$ with $\omega^2 = \dfrac{k}{m}$:

<KeyEquation
  tex="T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{m}{k}}"
  where="T | period of a mass–spring system | s
m | mass | kg
k | spring constant | N m⁻¹" />

A simple pendulum of length $l$ also has SHM, but **only for small angles**, with $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l}{g}}$.

<CommonMistake>
The period of SHM does **not** depend on the amplitude. A larger amplitude means a longer journey, but the object also moves faster, so each oscillation takes the same time. Do not confuse the two angular quantities either: $\omega = 2\pi f$, so a frequency of 2.5 Hz is $\omega = 15.7\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$, not 2.5.
</CommonMistake>

## Energy in simple harmonic motion

Energy moves back and forth between kinetic energy and potential energy. Without damping, the total energy stays constant.

<KeyEquation
  tex="E_\text{k} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2\left(x_0^2 - x^2\right) \qquad E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 \qquad E = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x_0^2"
  where="E_\text{k} | kinetic energy at displacement x | J
E_\text{p} | potential energy at displacement x | J
E | total energy | J
m | mass | kg
\omega | angular frequency | rad s⁻¹
x_0 | amplitude | m" />

For a mass on a spring, $m\omega^2 = k$, so the total energy is $E = \tfrac{1}{2}kx_0^2$. What the graphs look like:

- **Energy against displacement:** $E_\text{p}$ is an upright parabola with its minimum at $x = 0$, $E_\text{k}$ is an upside-down parabola with its maximum at $x = 0$, and the total energy is a horizontal line. The two curves add up to the line at every value of $x$.
- **Energy against time:** $E_\text{k}$ and $E_\text{p}$ both oscillate at **twice** the frequency of the oscillator itself, always adding up to the same total.

Because $E \propto x_0^2$, doubling the amplitude makes the total energy **four** times larger, even though the period stays the same.

<WorkedExample
  title="Kinetic energy at half the amplitude"
  marks={3}
  steps="At displacement $x = \tfrac{1}{2}x_0$: $E_\text{p} = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2\left(\tfrac{x_0}{2}\right)^2 = \tfrac{1}{4}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x_0^2\right) = \tfrac{1}{4}E$.
The total is constant, so $E_\text{k} = E - E_\text{p} = E - \tfrac{1}{4}E = \tfrac{3}{4}E$.
The fraction is $\tfrac{3}{4}$, or 75%."
>
An object oscillates with SHM with total energy $E$. What fraction of $E$ is kinetic energy when the displacement is half the amplitude?
</WorkedExample>

## Damping

In real oscillators, resistive forces such as air resistance and friction take energy from the system and transfer it to the surroundings as thermal energy.

<Definition term="Damping">
Damping is the loss of energy from an oscillating system, caused by resistive forces, so that the amplitude of the oscillations decreases.
</Definition>

There are three cases:

- **Light damping:** the object still oscillates, but the amplitude decreases gradually. The period is almost unchanged.
- **Critical damping:** the object returns to equilibrium in the **shortest possible time** and does **not** oscillate.
- **Heavy damping:** the object returns to equilibrium **slowly**, without oscillating.

Car suspensions and door closers are designed to be close to critical damping, so the system settles quickly without bouncing.

## Forced oscillations and resonance

A **free** oscillation happens when an object is displaced and then left alone. It oscillates at its **natural frequency** $f_0$. A **forced** oscillation happens when a periodic external force, with a **driving frequency** $f$, is applied. After the start-up, the system oscillates at the driving frequency.

<Definition term="Resonance">
Resonance occurs when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system. The amplitude of the forced oscillations is then at its maximum, because the system absorbs energy from the driver most efficiently.
</Definition>

A graph of amplitude against driving frequency has a peak at $f = f_0$. **More damping** makes the peak **lower and broader** (less sharp).

Resonance can be useful, for example tuning a radio circuit to a station or pushing a swing at the right moment. It can also be harmful, when it shakes buildings, bridges or machine parts apart. Damping is added to reduce the danger.
