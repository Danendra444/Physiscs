## Gravitational fields and field strength

Masses attract each other without touching. We describe this with a **field**: a region of space where something feels a force.

<Definition term="Gravitational field">
A gravitational field is a region of space in which a mass experiences a force because of its gravitational attraction to another mass.
</Definition>

<Definition term="Gravitational field strength">
The gravitational field strength at a point is the gravitational force exerted per unit mass on a small test mass placed at that point.
</Definition>

<KeyEquation
  tex="g = \dfrac{F}{m}"
  where="g | gravitational field strength | N kg⁻¹
F | gravitational force on the mass | N
m | the (small) test mass | kg" />

Field strength is a **vector**: it points in the direction of the force, towards the mass creating the field. Its unit N kg⁻¹ is the same as m s⁻², which is why $g$ is also the acceleration of free fall.

Near the surface of the Earth the field is nearly **uniform**: $g \approx 9.81\ \text{N kg}^{-1}$ everywhere in a small region, shown by parallel, equally spaced field lines. Over large distances the field is **radial**, with lines pointing towards the centre of the Earth, closer together where the field is stronger.

## Newton's law of gravitation

<Definition term="Newton's law of gravitation">
The gravitational force between two point masses is directly proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation.
</Definition>

<KeyEquation
  tex="F = \dfrac{GMm}{r^2}"
  where="F | gravitational force between the masses | N
G | gravitational constant, 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ | N m² kg⁻²
M, m | the two masses | kg
r | distance between the centres of the masses | m" />

The force on each mass is the same size and opposite in direction (Newton's third law). Spherical objects such as planets act as if all their mass were at their centre, so $r$ is the distance **between centres**.

Dividing $F = \dfrac{GMm}{r^2}$ by $m$ gives the field strength of a point mass (or a sphere) $M$ at distance $r$ from its centre:

<KeyEquation
  tex="g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}"
  where="g | gravitational field strength | N kg⁻¹
G | gravitational constant | N m² kg⁻²
M | mass creating the field | kg
r | distance from the centre of the mass | m" />

This is an **inverse-square law**: doubling the distance makes the field strength four times smaller.

<CommonMistake>
The distance $r$ is measured from the **centre** of the planet, not from its surface. For a satellite 400 km above the Earth, $r = R + 400\ \text{km}$, where $R$ is the radius of the Earth. Also remember that $g$ falls as $1/r^2$, not in proportion to $1/r$.
</CommonMistake>

## Orbits of satellites

A satellite in a **circular orbit** is in free fall around the planet. The gravitational force is the only force on it, and it provides the centripetal force:

$$
\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}
$$

The mass $m$ of the satellite cancels, so the orbit does not depend on how heavy the satellite is. Rearranging gives the orbital speed. Using $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ then gives the period:

<KeyEquation
  tex="v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}} \qquad\qquad T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}\,r^3"
  where="v | orbital speed | m s⁻¹
T | orbital period | s
r | radius of the orbit (from the centre of the planet) | m
M | mass of the planet | kg
G | gravitational constant | N m² kg⁻²" />

The second equation is **Kepler's third law**: $T^2 \propto r^3$. A graph of $T^2$ against $r^3$ is a straight line through the origin with gradient $\dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}$. Notice that higher orbits are **slower** and have a **longer period**.

<WorkedExample

### Geostationary orbits

A **geostationary satellite** stays above the same point on the Earth's surface. It is useful for satellite television and communications, because a dish can point at it without moving. To do this it must:

- orbit **above the equator**
- move in the **same direction** as the Earth spins (west to east)
- have a period of **24 hours**, the same as the Earth's rotation

Because the period is fixed, Kepler's third law fixes the radius of the orbit too.

<WorkedExample
  title="The geostationary orbit"
  marks={4}
  steps="The period is 24 hours: $T = 24 \times 3600 = 86\,400\ \text{s}$.
Rearrange $T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$ to get $r^3 = \dfrac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}$.
$r^3 = \dfrac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} \times (86\,400)^2}{4\pi^2} = 7.53 \times 10^{22}\ \text{m}^3$
$r = 4.22 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}$ from the centre of the Earth.
Height above the surface: $4.223 \times 10^{7} - 6.37 \times 10^{6} = 3.59 \times 10^{7}\ \text{m}$ (about 36 000 km), using the unrounded radius.
A sidereal day is 23 h 56 min. Using it instead changes $r$ by less than 0.2%."
>
Calculate the radius of the orbit of a geostationary satellite around the Earth, and its height above the surface. Mass of the Earth = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, radius of the Earth = 6.37 × 10⁶ m.
</WorkedExample>

<CommonMistake>
A satellite does not "escape gravity" or "float because there is no gravity". Gravity is what keeps it in orbit. The satellite is constantly falling towards the Earth but moving sideways fast enough that it keeps missing it. Answers that say "there is no gravity in space" score no marks.
</CommonMistake>

## Gravitational potential and energy

Because the gravitational force changes with distance, we need a different approach to talk about energy in a gravitational field. We choose **infinity** as the place where the potential is zero.

<Definition term="Gravitational potential">
The gravitational potential at a point is the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to that point.
</Definition>

<KeyEquation
  tex="\phi = -\dfrac{GM}{r}"
  where="\phi | gravitational potential | J kg⁻¹
G | gravitational constant | N m² kg⁻²
M | mass creating the field | kg
r | distance from the centre of the mass | m" />

Potential is **negative** everywhere, because gravity is attractive: the field itself does work as the mass comes in from infinity, so the potential falls below the zero at infinity. It is a **scalar**, so it has no direction. The closer you are to the mass, the more negative it is.

The gravitational potential energy of a mass $m$ at that point is $E_\text{p} = m\phi = -\dfrac{GMm}{r}$, and when a mass moves between two points, $\Delta E_\text{p} = m\,\Delta\phi$.

The field strength and the potential are linked: $g$ is the **negative of the gradient** of the potential–distance graph, $g = -\dfrac{\Delta\phi}{\Delta r}$. The minus sign shows that $g$ points in the direction in which the potential decreases.

<CommonMistake>
Do not drop the minus sign in $\phi = -\dfrac{GM}{r}$. Doing so gives a positive potential, which would mean the field pushes masses away. Remember also that "gaining potential energy" means becoming **less negative**: lifting a satellite to a higher orbit increases $E_\text{p}$, for example from $-6.25 \times 10^{7}$ to $-5.69 \times 10^{7}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$ per unit mass.
</CommonMistake>

<WorkedExample
  title="Energy to reach a higher orbit"
  marks={4}
  steps="The change in potential energy is $\Delta E_\text{p} = m\,\Delta\phi$, where $\Delta\phi = \phi_\text{final} - \phi_\text{initial}$.
$\Delta\phi = -\dfrac{GM}{r_2} - \left(-\dfrac{GM}{r_1}\right) = GM\left(\dfrac{1}{r_1} - \dfrac{1}{r_2}\right)$
$GM = 6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24} = 3.982 \times 10^{14}$, so $\Delta\phi = 3.982 \times 10^{14}\left(\dfrac{1}{6.37 \times 10^{6}} - \dfrac{1}{7.00 \times 10^{6}}\right) = 5.63 \times 10^{6}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$
$\Delta E_\text{p} = 1000 \times 5.63 \times 10^{6} = 5.63 \times 10^{9}\ \text{J}$
This is only the increase in potential energy. Getting into orbit also requires kinetic energy, so the total energy needed is larger."
>
A satellite of mass 1000 kg is lifted from the surface of the Earth to a circular orbit of radius 7.00 × 10⁶ m. Calculate the increase in its gravitational potential energy. Mass of the Earth = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, radius of the Earth = 6.37 × 10⁶ m.
</WorkedExample>

<ExamTip>
For orbit questions, start from the same line every time: "gravitational force provides the centripetal force", then write $\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$ (or $mr\omega^2$). Cross out the satellite's mass $m$, and substitute $v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}$ to get Kepler's third law. Examiners give marks for this reasoning even if you slip on the arithmetic.
</ExamTip>
