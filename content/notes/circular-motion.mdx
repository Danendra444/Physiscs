## Angular measure and angular velocity

An object moving along a circle is easiest to describe using **angles** instead of distances. Physicists measure angles in radians because it makes the equations simple.

<Definition term="Radian">
One radian is the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc that is equal in length to the radius of the circle.
</Definition>

The circumference of a circle is $2\pi r$, so one complete circle is $2\pi$ radians:

$$
360^\circ = 2\pi\ \text{rad} \qquad\text{so}\qquad 1\ \text{rad} \approx 57.3^\circ
$$

<KeyEquation
  tex="s = r\theta"
  where="s | length of the arc | m
r | radius of the circle | m
\theta | angle at the centre | rad" />

The **angular velocity** $\omega$ tells you how quickly the angle changes. If the object turns through an angle $\Delta\theta$ in a time $\Delta t$, then $\omega = \Delta\theta / \Delta t$. One full turn takes one period $T$, and $f = 1/T$ is the number of turns per second.

<KeyEquation
  tex="\omega = \dfrac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f"
  where="\omega | angular velocity | rad s⁻¹
\Delta\theta | angle turned through | rad
T | period (time for one revolution) | s
f | frequency (revolutions per second) | Hz" />

The speed along the circle is linked to the angular velocity by the radius:

<KeyEquation
  tex="v = r\omega"
  where="v | linear speed along the circle | m s⁻¹
r | radius of the circle | m
\omega | angular velocity | rad s⁻¹" />

Two points on the same turntable have the **same** $\omega$, but the point further from the centre has a larger $v$ because $v = r\omega$.

<CommonMistake>
Always set your calculator to **radians** for these equations, and convert revolutions per minute first:

$$
1\ \text{rev min}^{-1} = \dfrac{2\pi}{60}\ \text{rad s}^{-1}
$$

A very common lost mark is using $\omega = 45$ instead of converting 45 revolutions per minute to radians per second.
</CommonMistake>

## Centripetal acceleration

Velocity is a vector: it has a direction as well as a size. An object moving round a circle at constant speed keeps changing direction, so its **velocity is changing**, which means it is **accelerating**.

The acceleration points towards the centre of the circle, at right angles to the velocity, and has size:

<KeyEquation
  tex="a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = r\omega^2"
  where="a | centripetal acceleration | m s⁻²
v | linear speed | m s⁻¹
r | radius of the circle | m
\omega | angular velocity | rad s⁻¹" />

You can get the second form from the first by substituting $v = r\omega$: $\dfrac{(r\omega)^2}{r} = r\omega^2$.

<CommonMistake>
"Constant speed" does **not** mean "no acceleration". Speed is constant, but velocity is not, because its direction keeps changing. Circular motion at constant speed always has an acceleration towards the centre.
</CommonMistake>

<SelfCheck
  id="centripetal-acceleration"
  question="A car travels at 20 m s⁻¹ round a bend of radius 40 m. Calculate its centripetal acceleration."
  answer={10}
  unit="m s⁻²"
  tolerancePct={2}
  explanation="$a = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{20^2}{40} = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$, directed towards the centre of the bend." />

## Centripetal force

By Newton's second law, an acceleration needs a resultant force in the same direction. So an object in circular motion needs a resultant force towards the centre, called the **centripetal force**:

<KeyEquation
  tex="F = ma = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} = mr\omega^2"
  where="F | resultant force towards the centre | N
m | mass of the object | kg
v | linear speed | m s⁻¹
r | radius of the circle | m
\omega | angular velocity | rad s⁻¹" />

The centripetal force is **not a new kind of force**. It is the name for the resultant of whatever real forces point towards the centre. In each situation, decide which real force does the job:

- a bung whirled on a string: the **tension** in the string
- a car on a flat bend: the **friction** between the tyres and the road
- a satellite or planet in orbit: the **gravitational force**

Because the centripetal force is always at right angles to the velocity, it does **no work**. It changes the direction of the velocity but not the speed.

<CommonMistake>
Do not draw "centripetal force" as an extra arrow on a free-body diagram. Draw the real forces (tension, friction, weight, gravity…) and say that their resultant towards the centre is the centripetal force. Also avoid "centrifugal force": in this syllabus there is no outward force. An object that leaves the circle moves in a straight line along the tangent.
</CommonMistake>

  title="A car on a bend"
  marks={3}
  steps="Friction between the tyres and the road provides the centripetal force.
At the maximum speed the force is at its limit: $F = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, so $v^2 = \dfrac{Fr}{m}$.
$v^2 = \dfrac{6000 \times 50}{1200} = 250\ \text{m}^2\ \text{s}^{-2}$
$v = \sqrt{250} = 15.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (about 57 $\text{km h}^{-1}$)"
>
A car of mass 1200 kg drives round a flat circular bend of radius 50 m. The greatest sideways friction force the tyres can exert on the road is 6.0 kN.

Calculate the maximum speed at which the car can take the bend without skidding.
</WorkedExample>

<WorkedExample
  title="A ball in a vertical circle"
  marks={4}
  steps="At the lowest point the tension $T$ acts upwards, towards the centre, and the weight $mg$ acts downwards, away from the centre.
The resultant force towards the centre is the centripetal force: $T - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
$T = m\left(g + \dfrac{v^2}{r}\right) = 0.20 \times \left(9.81 + \dfrac{4.0^2}{0.60}\right)$
$T = 0.20 \times 36.48 = 7.3\ \text{N}$"
>
A ball of mass 0.20 kg on a string is swung in a vertical circle of radius 0.60 m. At the lowest point of the circle its speed is 4.0 m s⁻¹. Calculate the tension in the string at this point. Use $g = 9.81\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

The speed changes round a vertical circle, but at any instant the resultant force towards the centre is still $mv^2/r$.
</WorkedExample>

<ExamTip>
For a question that asks you to explain why an object moving at constant speed in a circle is accelerating, write three points: the **velocity changes** because its direction changes; so there is an **acceleration**; and it is directed **towards the centre**, so there must be a resultant force towards the centre. Then name the real force that provides it.
</ExamTip>
